\chapter{Multi-Timescale Memory and Stable Parametric Updates}\label{ch:multiscale}

\Cref{ch:ttt} introduced memories whose contents are \emph{parameters} written by gradient
steps while the model reads: the Titans memory, the fast weights of TTT-E2E, the in-place
down-projection of In-Place TTT. Once parameters become writable, two questions that implicit
memory never had to answer move to the centre of the design. The first is \emph{when}: how often
should each group of parameters be written? A Transformer has only two answers. Its KV cache is
rewritten at every token, and its weights are rewritten only during training, after which they
are frozen. Everything in between is unexplored. The second question is \emph{how}: a write into
a weight matrix that already stores useful knowledge can destroy that knowledge. This is the old
problem of catastrophic forgetting, now inside the model's memory. The survey of
\citet{zhoubian2026memory} treats the first question in its \S IV-D (``the temporal dynamics of
parameter evolution constitute an equally critical dimension'' of memory) and points, in
\S IV-E(f), to geometric constraints on the update rule as one answer to the second.

This chapter covers three 2025--2026 systems that answer these questions. \emph{Nested Learning}
and its architecture \emph{HOPE} \citep{behrouz2025nested} view a model as a stack of
optimization problems that run at different frequencies, and add a \emph{Continuum Memory
System} of MLP blocks updated every $C^{(1)} < C^{(2)} < \dots$ tokens. \emph{OLieRA}
\citep{cao2025olora} writes task-specific low-rank updates multiplicatively, inside a Lie group,
and keeps the updates of different tasks orthogonal. \emph{Muon-OGD} \citep{lu2026muonogd}
computes each update as the steepest-descent step under the spectral norm (the geometry of the
Muon optimizer) subject to linear constraints that forbid moving along directions that earlier
tasks rely on. \Cref{sec:c11-background} builds the background from scratch: forgetting, fast and
slow weights, optimizers as memories, continual learning, LoRA, Lie groups, and the Muon
optimizer with its Newton--Schulz iteration. One section per system follows
(\cref{sec:c11-nested,sec:c11-olieRA,sec:c11-muonogd}); \cref{sec:c11-hybrid} places all of them
in the survey's picture of multi-timescale hybrid memory, and \cref{sec:c11-comparison} compares
them.

A word on evidence. None of the three systems has an official code release that cites its
paper. Nested Learning has only an unofficial reproduction; OLieRA has no public code; Muon-OGD
has a repository on the first author's account whose README calls the method ``OSD'' and does
not cite the paper. Throughout the chapter we therefore keep three things apart: what the papers
state (from the survey, the abstracts, and a copy of the Nested Learning paper that ships with
the reproduction), what the mirrored code does, and our own formalizations, which we mark as
such.

\subsection{Catastrophic forgetting and the stability--plasticity dilemma}\label{sec:c11-forgetting}

Let a model with parameters $\vtheta$ meet tasks $1, 2, \dots, N$ one after another, each with
its own loss $\loss_n(\vtheta)$. After training on task $n$ we hold parameters $\vtheta_n$. The
\emph{forgetting} of an earlier task $j<n$ is how much its loss has risen since it was learned,
\begin{equation}
  F_{j,n} = \loss_j(\vtheta_n) - \loss_j(\vtheta_j).
  \label{eq:c11-forgetting}
\end{equation}
Neural networks trained by plain sequential gradient descent show large $F_{j,n}$, a
phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting or catastrophic interference
\citep{c11-mccloskey1989catastrophic,c11-french1999catastrophic}. A second-order Taylor expansion
shows where it comes from. A step $\bm{\Delta}$ taken for the new task changes an old loss by
\begin{equation}
  \loss_j(\vtheta + \bm{\Delta}) - \loss_j(\vtheta)
  \approx \nabla\loss_j(\vtheta)\T\bm{\Delta} + \tfrac12\,\bm{\Delta}\T\mH_j\,\bm{\Delta},
  \label{eq:c11-taylor}
\end{equation}
where $\mH_j$ is the Hessian of $\loss_j$. \emph{Plasticity} asks that the step reduce the new
loss, $\nabla\loss_n\T\bm{\Delta} < 0$. \emph{Stability} asks that both terms of
\cref{eq:c11-taylor} stay small for every $j<n$. Nothing forces the gradient of the new task to
respect the old tasks, so a step that is good for $\loss_n$ is usually bad for some $\loss_j$.
The \emph{stability--plasticity dilemma} names the trade-off: frequent, large updates adapt fast
but overwrite; rare, small updates preserve but adapt slowly. The survey puts it in exactly these
terms for explicit memory: ``frequent updates increase adaptability but risk overfitting to
transient signals, whereas slower updates preserve stability at the cost of responsiveness''
(\S IV-D(d)). Its remedy has three ingredients: regulate the \emph{magnitude}, the
\emph{frequency} and the \emph{scope} of updates. This chapter is organized around the last two
(frequency in \cref{sec:c11-nested}, scope and geometry in \cref{sec:c11-olieRA,sec:c11-muonogd}).

\begin{example}[Forgetting in two dimensions]\label{ex:c11-forgetting}
Take a linear model $\hat y = \vw\T\vx$ with $\vw \in \R^2$ and two one-example tasks,
\[
  \loss_A(\vw) = \tfrac12(\vw\T\va - 1)^2, \quad \va = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)\T,
  \qquad
  \loss_B(\vw) = \tfrac12(\vw\T\vb - 1)^2, \quad \vb = (1,0)\T.
\]
Learn A first and stop at the minimum-norm solution $\vw_A = \va$, so $\vw_A\T\va = 1$. Now run
gradient descent on B. Its gradient $(\vw\T\vb - 1)\vb$ always points along $\vb$, so only $w_1$
moves, and descent converges to $\vw = (1, 1/\sqrt2)\T$. Task B is solved, but
$\vw\T\va = (1 + 1/\sqrt2)/\sqrt2 \approx 1.207$, so $\loss_A$ rose from $0$ to
$\tfrac12(0.207)^2 \approx 0.021$. Suppose instead that we remove from every B-gradient its
component along $\va$, that is, we move only along
$\vb - (\vb\T\va)\va = (\tfrac12, -\tfrac12)\T$. Along this direction $\vw\T\va$ never changes.
Moving from $\vw_A$ by $s(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12)\T$ gives $\vw\T\vb = 1/\sqrt2 + s/2$, which
equals $1$ at $s = 2-\sqrt2 \approx 0.586$. The result $\vw \approx (1, 0.414)\T$ solves both
tasks exactly. This is orthogonal gradient projection, the idea behind
\cref{sec:c11-cl,sec:c11-muonogd}.
\end{example}

\subsection{Fast weights and slow weights}\label{sec:c11-fastslow}

Neural networks have long used parameters that change at different speeds.
\citet{schmidhuber1992fastweights} proposed a \emph{slow} network, trained by gradient descent,
that writes the weights of a second, \emph{fast} network while a sequence is processed; the fast
weights act as a short-term memory. \citet{ba2016fastweights} revived the idea for recurrent
networks: next to the slow recurrent weights they keep a fast matrix that decays and receives
Hebbian outer-product writes of the hidden state,
\begin{equation}
  \mA_t = \lambda\,\mA_{t-1} + \eta\,\vh_t\vh_t\T, \qquad 0<\lambda<1,
  \label{eq:c11-ba}
\end{equation}
and they read it as $\mA_t\vh$. With keys and values in place of $\vh_t$ this is linear attention
with a constant decay (\cref{ch:linear}); \citet{c02-schlag2021linear} made the equivalence between
linear Transformers and fast weight programmers explicit. The general pattern is a decomposition
$\mW_{\text{eff}}(t) = \mW_{\text{slow}} + \mW_{\text{fast}}(t)$, where the slow part changes
across training sequences and the fast part changes within one.

How long does a fast weight remember? Unroll \cref{eq:c11-ba}:
$\mA_t = \eta\sum_{s\le t}\lambda^{t-s}\vh_s\vh_s\T$. A write made $\tau$ steps ago is scaled by
$\lambda^\tau$. For an exponential moving average $\vm_t = \beta\vm_{t-1} + (1-\beta)\vg_t$ the
weights $(1-\beta)\beta^\tau$ sum to one, and the average age of what is stored is
\begin{equation}
  \bar\tau = \sum_{\tau\ge0}\tau(1-\beta)\beta^\tau
  = (1-\beta)\,\beta\,\frac{d}{d\beta}\sum_{\tau\ge0}\beta^\tau
  = (1-\beta)\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)^2} = \frac{\beta}{1-\beta}.
  \label{eq:c11-horizon}
\end{equation}
So $1/(1-\beta)$ is a natural \emph{timescale}: $\beta = 0.9$ remembers about $10$ steps,
$\beta = 0.99$ about $100$, $\beta = 0.999$ about $1000$. A second way to set a timescale is to
update a parameter only every $C$ steps, which gives it the \emph{update frequency}
$f = 1/C$. A parameter that is written every $C$ tokens with decay $\beta$ per write remembers on
the order of $C/(1-\beta)$ tokens. Both knobs appear in the systems below.

\subsection{Optimizers are associative memories}\label{sec:c11-optmem}

Nested Learning starts from a reinterpretation of ordinary training, which we now derive. Recall
that an associative memory stores key--value pairs so that a key retrieves its value
(\cref{ch:taxonomy}).

\paragraph{Gradient descent writes key--value pairs.} One gradient step is the solution of a
small optimization problem: move along the gradient but stay close to the current weights,
\begin{equation}
  \mW_{t+1} = \argmin_{\mW}\; \inner{\nabla\loss(\mW_t)}{\mW} + \frac{1}{2\eta}\norm{\mW - \mW_t}_F^2 .
  \label{eq:c11-gd-prox}
\end{equation}
Setting the gradient of the objective to zero gives
$\nabla\loss(\mW_t) + (\mW - \mW_t)/\eta = \bm{0}$, i.e.\ $\mW_{t+1} = \mW_t - \eta\nabla\loss(\mW_t)$.
For a linear layer $\vy = \mW\vx$ the chain rule gives $\nabla_{\mW}\loss = \vu\vx\T$ with
$\vu = \partial\loss/\partial\vy$, so
\begin{equation}
  \mW_{t+1} = \mW_t - \eta\,\vu_t\vx_t\T .
  \label{eq:c11-gd-write}
\end{equation}
This is an outer-product write, the same form as a linear-attention write $\mS_t = \mS_{t-1} +
\vv_t\vk_t\T$, with the input $\vx_t$ as the key and the scaled error $-\eta\vu_t$ as the value.
Training therefore stores, in $\mW$, a map from inputs to the error signals they caused.
\citet{behrouz2025nested} call $\vu_t$ the \emph{local surprise signal}: it measures how far the
layer's output was from what the objective wanted.

\paragraph{Momentum is a memory of past gradients.} Heavy-ball momentum keeps
$\vm_t = \beta\vm_{t-1} + \vg_t$ and steps $\mW_t = \mW_{t-1} - \eta\vm_t$. Unrolling,
$\vm_t = \sum_{s\le t}\beta^{t-s}\vg_s$: the momentum buffer is a decaying sum of all past
gradients, a fast weight in the sense of \cref{eq:c11-ba} whose ``key'' is constant. It is also
one gradient step (rate $1$) on its own small objective,
\begin{equation}
  \ell_t(\vm) = -\inner{\vm}{\vg_t} + \frac{1-\beta}{2}\norm{\vm}^2
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \vm_{t-1} - \nabla\ell_t(\vm_{t-1}) = \beta\vm_{t-1} + \vg_t = \vm_t .
  \label{eq:c11-momentum-obj}
\end{equation}
The first term rewards alignment with the newest gradient; the second makes the memory forget at
rate $1-\beta$. Training with momentum is thus a \emph{two-level} system: an inner memory
($\vm$) written from the gradient stream, and an outer memory ($\mW$) written from the inner
memory's output. Adam \citep{c01-kingma2015adam} adds a third memory, the moving average of
squared gradients, with its own timescale: with the common defaults $\beta_1 = 0.9$ and
$\beta_2 = 0.999$ the two moments remember roughly $10$ and $1000$ steps
(\cref{eq:c11-horizon}). An ordinary optimizer is already a multi-timescale memory.

\begin{keyidea}
Every stateful component of a learning system---attention's KV cache, a recurrent state, the
momentum buffer, the weights---is a memory that compresses some stream (tokens, gradients,
batches) by its own update rule. Two numbers characterize it: how often it is written (its
frequency) and how fast it forgets (its decay). Nested Learning makes these numbers explicit
design choices.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Continual learning: regularize, replay, project}\label{sec:c11-cl}

The continual-learning literature offers three families of defences against forgetting
(\cref{ch:taxonomy} discusses why they matter for writable memory).

\paragraph{Regularization.} Keep the parameters near the old solution, more strongly along
directions the old task cares about. Elastic weight consolidation \citep{c02-kirkpatrick2017ewc}
expands $\loss_A$ to second order around its optimum $\vtheta_A^*$, where the gradient vanishes,
so \cref{eq:c11-taylor} leaves only $\tfrac12(\vtheta-\vtheta_A^*)\T\mH_A(\vtheta-\vtheta_A^*)$.
Replacing the Hessian by the diagonal of the Fisher information $F_i$ gives the penalty
\begin{equation}
  \loss(\vtheta) = \loss_B(\vtheta) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i F_i\,(\theta_i - \theta^*_{A,i})^2 .
  \label{eq:c11-ewc}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Replay.} Store a few old examples and keep training on them. Gradient episodic memory
\citep{c02-lopezpaz2017gem} uses the stored examples differently: it computes their gradient
$\vg_{\text{old}}$ and, when the new gradient would increase the old loss
($\inner{\vg}{\vg_{\text{old}}} < 0$), projects $\vg$ to the closest vector that does not.

\paragraph{Projection.} Orthogonal gradient descent (OGD) \citep{c11-farajtabar2020ogd} removes
the need to store examples during the new task. After each task it stores a set of directions in
parameter space that the old task is sensitive to (gradients of the model's outputs on old
examples), orthonormalized by Gram--Schmidt into the columns of $\mQ \in \R^{p\times k}$. On the
new task it updates with the projected gradient
\begin{equation}
  \tilde\vg = (\mI - \mQ\mQ\T)\,\vg .
  \label{eq:c11-ogd}
\end{equation}
Two facts make this work. First, $\mQ\T\tilde\vg = \mQ\T\vg - (\mQ\T\mQ)\mQ\T\vg = \bm{0}$, so
for every stored direction $\vq$, a step $-\eta\tilde\vg$ changes the old quantity only at
second order: the first term of \cref{eq:c11-taylor} vanishes when $\nabla\loss_j \in
\operatorname{span}(\mQ)$. Second, $\tilde\vg$ is still a descent direction for the new task:
$\mP = \mI - \mQ\mQ\T$ is symmetric and idempotent ($\mP^2 = \mP$), so
$\vg\T\tilde\vg = \vg\T\mP\vg = \vg\T\mP\T\mP\vg = \norm{\tilde\vg}^2 \ge 0$. \Cref{ex:c11-forgetting}
was OGD with a single stored direction.

For a weight matrix a layer-wise variant is often more natural. Gradient projection memory (GPM)
\citep{c11-saha2021gpm} stores, for each linear layer $\vy = \mW\vx$, an orthonormal basis
$\mV \in \R^{n\times k}$ of the \emph{inputs} the layer saw on old tasks, and projects every
weight gradient on the input side, $\mG \mapsto \mG(\mI - \mV\mV\T)$. Then for any old input
$\vx \in \operatorname{span}(\mV)$,
\begin{equation}
  (\mW - \eta\mG(\mI - \mV\mV\T))\,\vx = \mW\vx - \eta\mG(\vx - \mV\mV\T\vx) = \mW\vx ,
  \label{eq:c11-gpm}
\end{equation}
exactly, not only to first order. This \emph{one-sided} projection will turn out to be the case in
which spectral-norm steepest descent with constraints has a closed form
(\cref{prop:c11-onesided}).

\subsection{Low-rank adaptation}\label{sec:c11-lora}

Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) \citep{c11-hu2022lora} fine-tunes a pretrained weight
$\mW_0 \in \R^{m\times n}$ by learning only a low-rank correction,
\begin{equation}
  \mW = \mW_0 + \mB\mA, \qquad \mB \in \R^{m\times r},\ \mA \in \R^{r\times n},\ r \ll \min(m,n),
  \label{eq:c11-lora}
\end{equation}
with $\mB$ initialized to zero so that training starts at $\mW_0$. The trainable parameter count
drops from $mn$ to $r(m+n)$: for $m = n = 4096$ and $r = 16$ that is $131{,}072$ instead of
$16{,}777{,}216$, about $0.8\%$. Two properties matter here. During training the correction can
be applied as $\mB(\mA\vx)$ in $\bigO((m+n)r)$ time per token without ever forming $\mB\mA$; after
training it can be merged into $\mW_0$ at no inference cost.

O-LoRA \citep{c11-wang2023olora} applies LoRA to continual learning. Each task $t$ gets its own
adapter $(\mB_t, \mA_t)$; adapters of earlier tasks are frozen, and the new adapter is pushed
into a subspace orthogonal to theirs by a penalty on the overlap of their factors. In our
row-space convention one way to write it is
\begin{equation}
  \loss_{\text{orth}} = \sum_{s<t}\norm{\mA_s\mA_t\T}_F^2 ,
  \label{eq:c11-olora}
\end{equation}
which vanishes exactly when the row spaces of $\mA_s$ and $\mA_t$ are orthogonal. Then
$\mB_t\mA_t\vx = \bm{0}$ for every $\vx$ in the row space of $\mA_s$: the new adapter does not
act on the input directions the old adapter used (the paper states the loss with its own factor
naming; see it for details). O-LoRA needs neither replayed data nor a task identifier at
inference, two properties OLieRA inherits (\cref{sec:c11-olieRA}).

\subsection{Lie groups, the exponential map and the Cayley transform}\label{sec:c11-lie}

OLieRA's central claim is that updates should be \emph{multiplicative} and live in a Lie group. We
need only a teaching-level view of these objects.

\paragraph{Groups of matrices.} A group is a set with an associative product, an identity and
inverses. The invertible $n\times n$ matrices form the general linear group $\mathrm{GL}(n)$; the
orthogonal matrices $\mathrm{O}(n) = \{\mQ : \mQ\T\mQ = \mI\}$ form a subgroup, and those with
determinant $1$ form the rotation group $\mathrm{SO}(n)$. These are \emph{Lie groups}: besides
being groups they are smooth surfaces (manifolds) inside the space of matrices, so one can move
along them continuously.

\paragraph{The Lie algebra.} The Lie algebra of a group is its tangent space at the identity: the
set of velocities $\dot\mQ(0)$ of smooth curves $\mQ(t)$ in the group with $\mQ(0) = \mI$. For
$\mathrm{O}(n)$, differentiate $\mQ(t)\T\mQ(t) = \mI$ at $t=0$:
$\dot\mQ(0)\T + \dot\mQ(0) = \bm{0}$. The Lie algebra of $\mathrm{O}(n)$ (and of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$)
is therefore the space of skew-symmetric matrices, $\{\mA : \mA\T = -\mA\}$, a vector space of
dimension $n(n-1)/2$. The point of the Lie algebra is that it is \emph{flat}: one can add its
elements and run unconstrained gradient descent on them.

\paragraph{The exponential map.} The matrix exponential
$\exp(\mA) = \sum_{k\ge0}\mA^k/k!$ carries the algebra into the group. For a skew-symmetric
$\mA$, $\exp(\mA)\T = \exp(\mA\T) = \exp(-\mA)$, and because $\mA$ and $-\mA$ commute,
$\exp(-\mA)\exp(\mA) = \exp(\bm{0}) = \mI$. So $\exp(\mA)$ is orthogonal, and
$\det\exp(\mA) = e^{\tr\mA} = 1$ places it in $\mathrm{SO}(n)$.

\begin{example}[Rotations in the plane]\label{ex:c11-rotation}
Let $\mA = \begin{psmallmatrix}0 & -\theta\\ \theta & 0\end{psmallmatrix}$. Then
$\mA^2 = -\theta^2\mI$, so the even powers are $(-1)^j\theta^{2j}\mI$ and the odd powers are
$(-1)^j\theta^{2j}\mA$. Summing the series,
$\exp(\mA) = \cos\theta\,\mI + \tfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\mA
= \begin{psmallmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{psmallmatrix}$, the
rotation by $\theta$. One unconstrained number in the algebra gives exactly one rotation.
\end{example}

\paragraph{The Cayley transform.} Computing a matrix exponential is expensive. The Cayley
transform
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{cay}(\mA) = (\mI - \tfrac12\mA)^{-1}(\mI + \tfrac12\mA)
  \label{eq:c11-cayley}
\end{equation}
is a cheaper map from skew-symmetric matrices to $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, needing one linear solve. It is
well defined because a skew-symmetric matrix has purely imaginary eigenvalues, so
$\mI - \tfrac12\mA$ is invertible. It is orthogonal: $\mI \pm \tfrac12\mA$ commute, and
$(\mI + \tfrac12\mA)\T = \mI - \tfrac12\mA$, so
$\operatorname{cay}(\mA)\T\operatorname{cay}(\mA) = (\mI - \tfrac12\mA)(\mI + \tfrac12\mA)^{-1}
(\mI - \tfrac12\mA)^{-1}(\mI + \tfrac12\mA) = \mI$. Expanding
$(\mI - \tfrac12\mA)^{-1} = \mI + \tfrac12\mA + \tfrac14\mA^2 + \tfrac18\mA^3 + \dots$ and multiplying
by $\mI + \tfrac12\mA$ gives $\mI + \mA + \tfrac12\mA^2 + \tfrac14\mA^3 + \dots$, which agrees with
$\exp(\mA) = \mI + \mA + \tfrac12\mA^2 + \tfrac16\mA^3 + \dots$ up to second order; the first
difference is $\tfrac{1}{12}\mA^3$. (Our NumPy check confirms orthogonality to $10^{-15}$ and that
the gap to $\exp$ shrinks by about $8\times$ each time $\mA$ is halved, as a third-order error
should.)

\paragraph{The Hadamard group.} The groups above act by matrix multiplication. A different Lie
group, and the one that public descriptions of OLieRA build on, uses the element-wise (Hadamard)
product $\had$. Let
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{G}_{\had} = \{\mW \in \R^{m\times n} : W_{ij} \ne 0 \text{ for all } i,j\}.
  \label{eq:c11-hadamard-group}
\end{equation}
Under $\had$ this set is a group: the identity is the all-ones matrix $\bm{1}$, the inverse of
$\mW$ is its element-wise reciprocal, and the product is commutative. Curves through $\bm{1}$ can
have any velocity, so the Lie algebra is all of $\R^{m\times n}$, and the exponential map is the
element-wise exponential, $[\exp_{\had}(\bm{\Delta})]_{ij} = e^{\Delta_{ij}}$. Because the group is
commutative, $\exp_{\had}(\bm{\Delta}_1)\had\exp_{\had}(\bm{\Delta}_2) = \exp_{\had}(\bm{\Delta}_1 +
\bm{\Delta}_2)$: in log-coordinates the group product is plain addition. A multiplicative update
$\mW' = \mW\had\exp_{\had}(\bm{\Delta})$ adds $\bm{\Delta}$ to $\log\abs{\mW}$ entry by entry and never
changes the sign of an entry. (The set has one connected piece per sign pattern; the exponential
map reaches only the positive piece, and multiplying by it keeps $\mW$ in its own piece.)

\subsection{Steepest descent under a norm: Muon and Newton--Schulz}\label{sec:c11-muon}

The optimizers of \cref{ch:foundations} (SGD, Adam) treat a weight matrix as a long vector. The
Muon optimizer \citep{c01-jordan2024muon} treats it as a linear map. The cleanest way to see the
difference is through \emph{steepest descent under a norm}: among all steps of size at most
$\eta$ in some norm, take the one that decreases the linearized loss the most,
\begin{equation}
  \bm{\Delta}^\star = \argmin_{\norm{\bm{\Delta}} \le \eta}\; \inner{\mG}{\bm{\Delta}},
  \qquad \inner{\mG}{\bm{\Delta}} = \tr(\mG\T\bm{\Delta}),
  \label{eq:c11-steepest}
\end{equation}
where $\mG \in \R^{m\times n}$ is the gradient (or a momentum of gradients). The answer depends on
the norm.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Frobenius norm.} By Cauchy--Schwarz, $\inner{\mG}{\bm{\Delta}} \ge
  -\norm{\mG}_F\norm{\bm{\Delta}}_F$, with equality at $\bm{\Delta}^\star = -\eta\,\mG/\norm{\mG}_F$:
  normalized gradient descent.
  \item \textbf{Element-wise max norm.} Each entry can move by $\eta$ independently, so
  $\bm{\Delta}^\star = -\eta\operatorname{sign}(\mG)$: sign descent, the limit of Adam with no
  averaging.
  \item \textbf{Spectral norm} $\norm{\bm{\Delta}}_2 = \sigma_{\max}(\bm{\Delta})$, the largest factor
  by which $\bm{\Delta}$ can stretch a vector. Write the thin SVD $\mG = \mU\mSigma\mV\T =
  \sum_i\sigma_i\vu_i\vv_i\T$ over its $r$ nonzero singular values. (In this section $\mU,\mV$
  denote singular vectors, not the stacked values of attention.) Then
  \begin{equation}
    \inner{\mG}{\bm{\Delta}} = \sum_i\sigma_i\,\vu_i\T\bm{\Delta}\vv_i \ge -\sum_i\sigma_i\norm{\bm{\Delta}}_2
    = -\eta\norm{\mG}_* ,
    \label{eq:c11-nuclear}
  \end{equation}
  because $\abs{\vu\T\bm{\Delta}\vv} \le \norm{\bm{\Delta}}_2$ for unit vectors. Here
  $\norm{\mG}_* = \sum_i\sigma_i$ is the nuclear norm, the dual of the spectral norm. Equality holds
  for $\bm{\Delta}^\star = -\eta\,\mU\mV\T$, since then $\vu_i\T\mU\mV\T\vv_i = 1$ for every $i$.
\end{itemize}
The matrix $\mU\mV\T$ (built from the nonzero singular values only) is the \emph{matrix sign}
$\operatorname{msign}(\mG)$, also called the orthogonal polar factor, because
$\mG = (\mU\mV\T)(\mV\mSigma\mV\T)$ splits $\mG$ into a partial isometry times a positive
semidefinite matrix. Replacing $\mG$ by $\operatorname{msign}(\mG)$ sets every singular value to
$1$: the update moves every input direction that the gradient touches by the same amount,
instead of letting the few dominant directions of $\mG$ take almost all of the step.

\paragraph{Muon.} For each 2-D weight matrix, Muon keeps a momentum
$\mM_t = \mu\mM_{t-1} + \mG_t$ and steps $\mW_t = \mW_{t-1} - \eta\,s\,\operatorname{msign}(\mM_t)$,
with a scale $s$ that depends on the shape. Embeddings, normalization gains and biases are left to
AdamW. Computing an SVD at every step would be too slow, so Muon approximates
$\operatorname{msign}$ with a few Newton--Schulz iterations \citep{c01-higham2008functions}.

\paragraph{Newton--Schulz.} Start from $\mX_0 = \mG/\norm{\mG}_F$, whose singular values lie in
$(0,1]$, and iterate
\begin{equation}
  \mX_{k+1} = \tfrac12\mX_k(3\mI - \mX_k\T\mX_k).
  \label{eq:c11-ns3}
\end{equation}
If $\mX_k = \mU f_k(\mSigma)\mV\T$ then $\mX_k\T\mX_k = \mV f_k(\mSigma)^2\mV\T$ and
$\mX_{k+1} = \mU\,\tfrac12 f_k(\mSigma)(3 - f_k(\mSigma)^2)\,\mV\T$. The singular vectors never
change, and each singular value follows the scalar map
$\varphi(\sigma) = \tfrac32\sigma - \tfrac12\sigma^3$. Its non-negative fixed points are $0$ and $1$. Near $1$,
$\varphi(1+\epsilon) = 1 - \tfrac32\epsilon^2 - \tfrac12\epsilon^3$: the error is squared at each
step (quadratic convergence). Near $0$, $\varphi(\sigma) \approx 1.5\,\sigma$: small singular
values grow geometrically until they reach the fast regime. Any $\sigma \in (0, \sqrt3)$
converges to $1$, and zero singular values stay at zero, which is why the iteration computes the
canonical $\mU\mV\T$ over the nonzero singular values. In our NumPy check on a $64\times 32$
Gaussian matrix, the distance to the exact polar factor is $0.23$ after $5$ iterations,
$1.4\times10^{-6}$ after $10$, and at machine precision after $20$. Each iteration costs two
matrix products, $\bigO(mn\min(m,n))$ operations, and runs well in low precision.

Practical Muon uses a quintic iteration $\mX \leftarrow a\mX + b(\mX\mX\T)\mX + c(\mX\mX\T)^2\mX$
whose coefficients are tuned to make small singular values grow as fast as possible
(\cref{lst:c11-muon-ns5}). It deliberately does not converge to exactly $1$: on the same test
matrix, five quintic steps leave the singular values in $[0.68, 1.11]$. According to the docstring
of the excerpted function, this ``turns out not to hurt model performance at all'' relative to the
exact $\mU\mV\T$.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Excerpt from \texttt{repos/\allowbreak awslabs\_\_hybrid-model-factory/\allowbreak training/\allowbreak src/\allowbreak hmf/\allowbreak third\_party/\allowbreak muon/\allowbreak muon.py} (the widely used quintic Newton--Schulz of Muon; docstring elided)},label={lst:c11-muon-ns5}]
def zeropower_via_newtonschulz5(G: "torch.Tensor", steps: int) -> "torch.Tensor":
    # ...
    assert len(G.shape) == 2
    a, b, c = (3.4445, -4.7750, 2.0315)
    X = G.bfloat16()
    if G.size(0) > G.size(1):
        X = X.T
    # Ensure spectral norm is at most 1
    X = X / (X.norm() + 1e-7)
    # Perform the NS iterations
    for _ in range(steps):
        A = X @ X.T
        B = b * A + c * A @ A  # adapted from suggestion by @jxbz, @leloykun, and @YouJiacheng
        X = a * X + B @ X

    if G.size(0) > G.size(1):
        X = X.T
    return X
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One Muon step for a weight matrix $\mW \in \R^{m\times n}$.}\label{alg:c11-muon}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require weight $\mW$, gradient $\mG$, momentum buffer $\mM$, rate $\eta$, momentum $\mu$, NS steps $K$
\State $\mM \gets \mu\mM + \mG$ \Comment{memory of past gradients, timescale $1/(1-\mu)$}
\State $\mX \gets \mM/\norm{\mM}_F$ \Comment{singular values now in $(0,1]$}
\For{$k = 1,\dots,K$}
  \State $\mX \gets \tfrac12\mX(3\mI - \mX\T\mX)$ \Comment{or the quintic variant of \cref{lst:c11-muon-ns5}}
\EndFor
\State $\mW \gets \mW - \eta\, s(m,n)\,\mX$ \Comment{$\mX \approx \operatorname{msign}(\mM)$}
\Ensure updated $\mW$, $\mM$. Cost $\bigO(K\,mn\min(m,n))$; extra state: $\mM$ ($mn$ numbers).
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{pitfall}[title={Unofficial code}]

describes it as a ``mechanism-level reproduction'' and states that it does not aim at
paper-scale results. It pins its equation references to a full-length copy of the paper
(\code{google_papers/Nested_Learning_Full_Paper/...}) that is \emph{not} in our mirror; the mirror
contains only the shorter NeurIPS camera-ready text at
\repofile{repos/kmccleary3301__nested_learning/google_papers/Nested_Learning/Nested_Learning.md}.
Where we describe the self-modifying Titans module in detail, we describe the reproduction's
code, which cites the full paper's Eqs.~83--97 that we could not check.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Motivation}\label{sec:c11-nl-motivation}

The paper motivates Nested Learning with an analogy to anterograde amnesia, a condition in which
a person keeps old memories but cannot form new long-term ones. A deployed Transformer is in the
same state: what it reads stays in the attention window (short-term memory) and never reaches
the MLP weights (long-term memory), which were last written at the end of pre-training. The
Titans memory of \cref{ch:ttt} adds one in-between level, a memory written at test time. Nested
Learning generalizes this in three steps: (i) describe every component of a model and of its
optimizer as an associative memory with its own \emph{context flow} (the stream it compresses)
and its own update frequency; (ii) add a whole spectrum of levels between ``every token'' and
``never'', the \emph{Continuum Memory System} (CMS); (iii) make the sequence model learn its own
update rule (\emph{self-modifying Titans}). HOPE is self-modifying Titans followed by a CMS. The
survey summarizes the idea as replacing ``a binary distinction between backbone and memory'' with
``a spectrum of temporal plasticity'' (\S IV-D(b)).

\begin{taxonomy}
Following \citet{zhoubian2026memory}: \textbf{representation} explicit (designated parameter
subsets---CMS blocks and Titans memories---are operated as writable memory);
\textbf{update dynamics} online (these subsets are written during inference, at several
frequencies); \textbf{persistence} long-term (Table~I lists Nested Learning as Online, Long-Term,
``Multi-Timescale Updated Modules''); \textbf{update rule} optimization-based writing (Table~II,
whose listed risks are drift, instability and objective mismatch).
\end{taxonomy}

\subsection{Formulation}\label{sec:c11-nl-formulation}

\paragraph{Levels.} The paper adopts a general definition of associative memory: given keys
$\mathcal{K}$ and values $\mathcal{V}$, a memory is an operator $\mathcal{M}$ learned by
$\mathcal{M}^* = \argmin_{\mathcal{M}}\tilde\loss(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K});\mathcal{V})$ for some
inner objective $\tilde\loss$. Keys and values need not be tokens; they can be gradients, chunks,
or error signals. To order the many memories inside a model it defines:

\begin{definition}[Update frequency and levels \citep{behrouz2025nested}]\label{def:c11-frequency}
Take one update step on one data point (one token) as the unit of time. The \emph{frequency}
$f_A$ of a component $A$ is its number of updates per unit of time. $A$ is \emph{faster} than
$B$, written $A \succ B$, if $f_A > f_B$, or if $f_A = f_B$ and computing $B$'s state at time
$t$ requires $A$'s state at time $t$. Sorting components by $\succ$ groups them into
\emph{levels}; components in one level share a frequency, and higher levels are slower.
\end{definition}

Each component optimizes its own objective over its own context with its own gradient flow; no
gradient passes between levels inside this decomposition. \Cref{sec:c11-optmem} already gave
three examples. Plain gradient descent is a one-level memory that maps inputs $\vx_t$ to their
local surprise $\vu_t$ (\cref{eq:c11-gd-write}). Momentum adds a faster level, the momentum
buffer (\cref{eq:c11-momentum-obj}): it is written as often as the weights, but computing the
weights at step $t$ needs the momentum at step $t$, so it ranks as faster. A linear-attention layer trained by gradient descent is
two levels: the inner memory $\mS_t = \mS_{t-1} + \vv_t\vk_t\T$ is one gradient step (rate $1$) on
$\tilde\loss(\mS) = -\inner{\mS\vk_t}{\vv_t}$, whose gradient is $-\vv_t\vk_t\T$, and runs once per
token; the outer projections $\mW_Q, \mW_K, \mW_V$ run once per training batch. At deployment the
outer level stops: its frequency drops to zero.

\paragraph{Deep optimizers.} Seeing the optimizer as a memory suggests better optimizers in the
same way that better memories suggest better sequence models. The paper lists several. With a
value matrix $\mP_i$ instead of a constant, the momentum objective becomes
$\inner{\vm\,\nabla\loss\T}{\mP_i}$ and gradient descent on it gives preconditioned momentum,
$\vm_{i+1} = \alpha\vm_i - \eta\mP_i\nabla\loss$. Replacing the dot-product objective by an
$\ell_2$ regression objective gives a delta-rule momentum. Replacing the linear momentum by an
MLP gives \emph{deep momentum gradient descent}. Applying a nonlinearity to the momentum's output
and choosing Newton--Schulz as that nonlinearity recovers Muon (\cref{sec:c11-muon}). The same
$\ell_2$ idea applied to the weights themselves gives the update HOPE uses inside its memories.
Ask the layer to map its input $\vx_t$ to a target $\bm{c}_t$ with one unit-rate step on
$\tfrac12\norm{\mW\vx_t - \bm{c}_t}^2$, whose gradient is $(\mW\vx_t - \bm{c}_t)\vx_t\T$:
\begin{equation}
  \mW_{t+1} = \mW_t - (\mW_t\vx_t - \bm{c}_t)\vx_t\T = \mW_t(\mI - \vx_t\vx_t\T) + \bm{c}_t\vx_t\T .
  \label{eq:c11-dgd}
\end{equation}
With $\bm{c}_t = -\eta\vu_t$ this is the paper's variant of gradient descent,
$\mW_{t+1} = \mW_t(\mI - \vx_t\vx_t\T) - \eta\nabla_{\vy}\loss\,\vx_t\T$. For a unit-norm $\vx_t$ the
factor $\mI - \vx_t\vx_t\T$ first erases what $\mW$ currently returns for $\vx_t$, then writes the
new association: it is the delta rule of \cref{ch:delta} applied to weights. With a retention gate
$\alpha_t$ and a step $\eta_t$ the factor becomes $\alpha_t\mI - \eta_t\vx_t\vx_t\T$, the
``rank-1 preconditioner'' of the reproduction.

\paragraph{The Continuum Memory System.} A Transformer block has one MLP, written only in
pre-training. The CMS replaces it by a chain of $k$ MLP blocks, one per frequency,
\begin{equation}
  \vy_t = \mathrm{MLP}^{(k)}\bigl(\mathrm{MLP}^{(k-1)}(\cdots\mathrm{MLP}^{(1)}(\vx_t))\bigr),
  \label{eq:c11-cms-chain}
\end{equation}
(normalizations omitted) where block $\ell$ has parameters $\vtheta^{(\ell)}$ and an update period
(chunk size) $C^{(1)} < C^{(2)} < \dots < C^{(k)}$ tokens, inversely proportional to its frequency.
Block $\ell$ is written only at the end of each of its chunks, with the sum of the error terms
of the tokens in that chunk:
\begin{equation}
  \vtheta^{(\ell)}_{i+1} = \vtheta^{(\ell)}_{i} -
  \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle\sum_{t=i-C^{(\ell)}+1}^{i}\eta_t^{(\ell)}\, f\bigl(\vtheta^{(\ell)}_{i};\vx_t\bigr)
      & \text{if } i \equiv 0 \pmod{C^{(\ell)}},\\[2mm]
    \bm{0} & \text{otherwise,}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:c11-cms-update}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is ``the error component of an arbitrary optimizer'', for example the gradient of a
local loss. (The paper writes the sum from $i-C^{(\ell)}$; we index the $C^{(\ell)}$ tokens of the
chunk.) A standard Transformer block is the special case $k = 1$ with the block never written
after training. The paper's reading of \cref{eq:c11-cms-update} is that each block compresses its
own context, at its own granularity, into its parameters: the slow blocks see the stream only
through sums over long chunks and therefore hold more abstract, longer-lived knowledge; the fast
blocks track the recent past.

Two properties of \cref{eq:c11-cms-update} are worth making precise.

\begin{proposition}[Recurrent and chunkwise forms agree]\label{prop:c11-cms-chunk}
Within one chunk of level $\ell$ the parameters $\vtheta^{(\ell)}$ are constant. Hence (a) every
token of the chunk is read with the start-of-chunk parameters, and (b) the error terms
$f(\vtheta^{(\ell)}_i;\vx_t)$ of the chunk can be computed in parallel. For a linear layer
$\vy = \mW\vx$ with per-token error signals $\bm{\delta}_t$, the accumulated update is a single
matrix product: $\sum_{t\in\text{chunk}}\bm{\delta}_t\vx_t\T = \bm{\Delta}_{[c]}\T\mX_{[c]}$, where
$\mX_{[c]}, \bm{\Delta}_{[c]} \in \R^{C\times d}$ stack the inputs and error signals as rows.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) holds because \cref{eq:c11-cms-update} writes only at chunk ends. For (b), each term depends on
$\vtheta^{(\ell)}_i$, which is fixed, and on token $t$'s own input, so the terms are independent.
The matrix identity is the definition of the product: entry $(a,b)$ of $\bm{\Delta}_{[c]}\T\mX_{[c]}$
is $\sum_t\delta_{t,a}x_{t,b}$, which is entry $(a,b)$ of $\sum_t\bm{\delta}_t\vx_t\T$.
\end{proof}

The chunkwise form is what makes CMS trainable on accelerators. One subtlety: the \emph{inputs}
of a slow level are the outputs of faster levels, which may be written several times inside the
slow level's chunk. A parallel implementation must therefore process the sequence in blocks of
the \emph{smallest} period $C^{(1)}$, so that every level reads consistent parameters; the
reproduction does exactly this. Our NumPy check runs a three-level CMS with periods $(2, 8, 32)$
over $64$ tokens both token by token with buffers and in base-chunk blocks with one batched
gradient per level chunk: the parameters agree to $6\times10^{-17}$ and the outputs to
$4\times10^{-16}$, and the levels are written $32$, $8$ and $2$ times.

\paragraph{Where the error signal comes from.} \Cref{eq:c11-cms-update} needs an error term for
every token. The reproduction uses a \emph{teach signal}: the gradient of the language-modelling
loss with respect to a hidden state. At the top of the network, with tied embeddings
$\mW_E \in \R^{\abs{\mathcal{V}}\times d}$, logits $\vz_t = \mW_E\vh_t$ and mean cross-entropy over
$N$ supervised positions, $\partial\loss/\partial\vz_t = (\bm{p}_t - \ve_{y_t})/N$ with
$\bm{p}_t = \softmax(\vz_t)$ and $\ve_{y_t}$ the one-hot target, so
\begin{equation}
  \bm{\delta}_t = \frac{\partial\loss}{\partial\vh_t} = \frac{1}{N}\,\mW_E\T(\bm{p}_t - \ve_{y_t}).
  \label{eq:c11-teach}
\end{equation}
With per-layer teach signals enabled, $\bm{\delta}^{(l)}_t$ is instead the autograd gradient of
the loss with respect to the output of block $l$. A CMS level then takes one step on the local
loss $\sum_t\norm{\hat\vy_t - \operatorname{sg}(\hat\vy_t - \bm{\delta}_t)}^2$, where
$\hat\vy_t$ is the level's output and $\operatorname{sg}$ stops gradients. The loss value is just
$\sum_t\norm{\bm{\delta}_t}^2$, but its gradient with respect to $\hat\vy_t$ is
$2(\hat\vy_t - \hat\vy_t + \bm{\delta}_t) = 2\bm{\delta}_t$, so the parameter gradient is
$2\sum_t\mathbf{J}_t\T\bm{\delta}_t$ with $\mathbf{J}_t$ the Jacobian of the level's output: the
teach signal, back-propagated through this one block only. (Our finite-difference check agrees
to $9\times10^{-11}$.) In the reproduction every CMS level of a block receives the \emph{same}
$\bm{\delta}_t$, the error at the block output. Because each CMS level is residual,
$\vx + \mathrm{MLP}(\vx)$, the identity path carries an output error unchanged to the levels below,
so this is the approximation that ignores the MLP part of the higher levels' Jacobians.

\paragraph{HOPE: self-modifying Titans plus CMS.} Recall Titans from \cref{ch:ttt}. Its neural
memory $\mathcal{M}$ is an MLP written at test time by surprise-driven gradient steps with
momentum and decay,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{M}_t = (1-\alpha_t)\mathcal{M}_{t-1} + \mS_t, \qquad
  \mS_t = \eta_t\mS_{t-1} - \theta_t\nabla\ell(\mathcal{M}_{t-1};\vx_t), \qquad
  \ell = \norm{\mathcal{M}(\vk_t) - \vv_t}^2 ,
  \label{eq:c11-titans}
\end{equation}
with $\vk_t = \mW_K\vx_t$, $\vv_t = \mW_V\vx_t$ and read-out $\vo_t = \mathcal{M}_t(\vq_t)$
\citep{behrouz2025titans}. The projections $\mW_K, \mW_V$ and the networks that produce the gates
are slow parameters: they change only in pre-training. Self-modifying Titans makes them fast too.
As implemented in the reproduction, it keeps one small memory per quantity,
$\mathcal{M}_\square$ for $\square \in \{k, v, \eta, \alpha, \text{mem}\}$ (plus $q$ if the query is
made adaptive), each a two-layer residual MLP
$\mathcal{M}_\square(\vz) = \vz + \mW^\square_1\,\mathrm{GELU}(\mW^\square_2\vz)$ with hidden
width $d$ (the scalar-output memories for $\eta$ and $\alpha$ have no residual path). For each
token, after a causal depthwise convolution of width $4$ over the inputs,
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  \vk_t = \mathcal{M}_k(\vx_t),\quad
  \vv_t = \mathcal{M}_v(\vx_t),\quad
  \eta_t = c\cdot\mathrm{softplus}(\mathcal{M}_\eta(\vx_t)),\quad
  \alpha_t = \sigmoid(\mathcal{M}_\alpha(\vx_t)),\\
  \vq_t = \mW_q\vx_t,\qquad
  \vo_t = \mathcal{M}_{\text{mem}}(\vq_t),
\end{gathered}
  \label{eq:c11-selfmod-read}
\end{equation}
with a small constant $c$ (\code{eta_scale}, default $10^{-3}$); keys and queries are optionally
normalized to unit length (\code{qk_l2_norm}), which makes the preconditioner below an exact erase. Every memory is trained in
context on a target it generates itself, its own transform of the value,
\begin{equation}
  \ell_\square(\vtheta_\square) = \norm{\mathcal{M}_\square(\vk_t) - \operatorname{sg}(\mathcal{M}_\square(\vv_t))}^2 ,
  \label{eq:c11-selfmod-loss}
\end{equation}
and is written with the delta-rule step of \cref{eq:c11-dgd} on its input-side matrix and a
decayed gradient step on its output-side matrix:
\begin{equation}
  \mW^\square_2 \leftarrow \mW^\square_2(\alpha_t\mI - \eta_t\vk_t\vk_t\T) - \eta_t\nabla_{\mW_2}\ell_\square,
  \qquad
  \mW^\square_1 \leftarrow \alpha_t\mW^\square_1 - \eta_t\nabla_{\mW_1}\ell_\square .
  \label{eq:c11-selfmod-update}
\end{equation}
The memory that produces the output, $\mathcal{M}_{\text{mem}}$, and the memories that produce its
keys, values and gates are written on two different chunk schedules, $C_m$ and $C_o$; the
reproduction's self-modifying preset uses $C_o = 8$ and $C_m = 64$ (``other memories update more
often than $M_{\text{memory}}$''). Within a chunk, all per-token gradients are evaluated at the
chunk-start state, in parallel, and then applied token by token with the preconditioner---the
chunkwise trick of \cref{prop:c11-cms-chunk} applied to Titans. A HOPE block is this module
followed by a CMS (\cref{fig:c11-hope}); a simpler HOPE-Attention block uses softmax attention
followed by a CMS.

\subsection{Algorithms and complexity}\label{sec:c11-nl-alg}

\Cref{alg:c11-cms} gives the CMS forward pass with online writes, in the buffered form the
reproduction uses; \cref{alg:c11-two-pass} shows how such writes are combined with ordinary
training.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{CMS forward pass with online multi-frequency writes (one sequence).}\label{alg:c11-cms}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require block inputs $\vx_{1:T}$; error signals $\bm{\delta}_{1:T}$; levels $\ell = 1..k$ with
parameters $\vtheta^{(\ell)}$, periods $C^{(\ell)}$, level optimizers $\mathrm{Opt}^{(\ell)}$
\State $C_{\min} \gets \min_\ell C^{(\ell)}$; empty buffers $\mathcal{B}^{(\ell)}$ for all $\ell$
\For{$c = 0, C_{\min}, 2C_{\min}, \dots < T$}
  \State $\mH \gets \vx_{c+1:c+C_{\min}}$
  \For{$\ell = 1,\dots,k$} \Comment{read: all levels at their current parameters}
    \State $\mZ^{(\ell)} \gets \mH$; \quad $\mH \gets \mH + \mathrm{MLP}(\mH;\vtheta^{(\ell)})$
  \EndFor
  \State emit $\vy_{c+1:c+C_{\min}} \gets \mH$
  \For{$\ell = 1,\dots,k$} \Comment{write: only levels whose chunk is complete}
    \State append $(\mZ^{(\ell)}, \bm{\delta}_{c+1:c+C_{\min}})$ to $\mathcal{B}^{(\ell)}$
    \While{$\mathcal{B}^{(\ell)}$ holds at least $C^{(\ell)}$ tokens}
      \State pop the oldest $C^{(\ell)}$ tokens $(\mZ, \bm{\Delta})$
      \State $\vg \gets \nabla_{\vtheta}\sum_t\inner{\bm{\delta}_t}{\mathrm{MLP}(\vz_t;\vtheta)}\big|_{\vtheta = \vtheta^{(\ell)}}$ \Comment{parallel over the chunk}
      \State $\vtheta^{(\ell)} \gets \vtheta^{(\ell)} - \eta^{(\ell)}\,\mathrm{Opt}^{(\ell)}(\vg)$
    \EndWhile
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\Ensure outputs $\vy_{1:T}$; leftover buffer contents are carried to the next call or flushed at the end
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One outer training step with stop-gradient online writes (the reproduction's default \texttt{two\_pass\_stopgrad\_updates}).}\label{alg:c11-two-pass}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require sequence $\vx_{1:T}$; online chunk size $C$ (default $C_{\min}$ of the CMS); outer optimizer
\State re-initialize fast state (per-context copies or deltas of the memories); zero outer gradients
\For{each chunk $[c]$ of $C$ tokens}
  \State forward with current fast state; loss $\loss_{[c]}$ \Comment{differentiable read}
  \State per-layer teach signals $\bm{\delta}^{(l)} \gets \partial\loss_{[c]}/\partial(\text{block } l \text{ output})$
  \State $\loss_{[c]}$.backward() \Comment{accumulate outer gradients across chunks}
  \State \textbf{with no gradient:} run the blocks again with $\bm{\delta}^{(l)}$ \Comment{write: CMS (\cref{alg:c11-cms}) and Titans memories}
\EndFor
\State clip outer gradients; outer optimizer step (Muon hybrid, AdamW or M3)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Complexity.} Let each CMS level be an MLP $d \to hd \to d$. Reading costs $\bigO(hd^2)$
per token and level, so $\bigO(Tkhd^2)$ per sequence and layer. Writing level $\ell$ needs one
backward pass through that level for every token in its chunks; since every token belongs to
exactly one chunk of each level, writing also costs $\bigO(Tkhd^2)$, \emph{independent} of the
periods. The periods change only how many parameter writes happen ($T/C^{(\ell)}$ per level) and
how much the buffers hold ($\bigO(C^{(\ell)}d)$ inputs and error signals per level, which is what
limits long periods in practice). The state is the CMS parameters themselves, $2khd^2$ numbers per
layer, plus optimizer state. Self-modifying Titans with hidden width $d$ costs $\bigO(d^2)$ per
token and memory for the read, the per-token gradients and the preconditioned write
($\mW_2(\alpha\mI - \eta\vk\vk\T)$ is a rank-one correction, computed in $\bigO(d^2)$), so
$\bigO(Td^2)$ per layer with about $6\times2d^2$ numbers of state. Decoding is the same
computation with $T = 1$ per step.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Excerpt from \texttt{repos/\allowbreak kmccleary3301\_\_nested\_learning/\allowbreak src/\allowbreak nested\_learning/\allowbreak hope/\allowbreak block.py}: the gradient-shaping loss whose gradient with respect to the prediction is $2\bm{\delta}$},label={lst:c11-chunkloss}]
def _chunk_loss(
    prediction: torch.Tensor,
    delta_target: torch.Tensor,
    mask_f: torch.Tensor,
    *,
    reduction: str,
    differentiable_target: bool = False,
) -> torch.Tensor:
    if differentiable_target:
        target = prediction.detach() - delta_target
    else:
        target = (prediction.detach() - delta_target).detach()
    diff_sq = (prediction - target).pow(2)
    masked = diff_sq * mask_f
    if reduction == "mean":
        return masked.sum() / mask_f.sum().clamp(min=1.0)
    if reduction == "sum":
        return masked.sum()
    raise ValueError(f"Unsupported cms_chunk_reduction={reduction}")
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{implnote}[title={In the code: the level optimizer projects out the context direction}]
Each level's optimizer is a \code{DeepMomentum} module (\code{optim/deep.py}) applied through
\code{LevelOptimizerManager}, which subtracts \code{lr * update} from each parameter. The pilot
configs select \code{variant: nl_l2_precond}, which the docs call a ``best-effort'' mapping of the
paper's $\ell_2$ optimizer: the gradient is projected orthogonally to the unit context vector
$\hat\vx$ along its last dimension, $\mG \mapsto \mG(\mI - \hat\vx\hat\vx\T)$
(\cref{lst:c11-nlprecond}), and then averaged by an EMA with $\beta = 0.9$
(\code{grad_avg = beta * grad_avg + (1 - beta) * update}). The projection is applied only to
tensors whose last dimension equals $d$: the input-side matrix of \code{Linear(d, 4d)}, but also
$d$-dimensional vectors such as the LayerNorm parameters and the output bias; the output-side
matrix and the hidden bias skip it. Note that this is the input-side, GPM-style projection of
\cref{eq:c11-gpm} with a single direction (the chunk's mean input), applied to the gradient, which
is not the same operation as the weight-side factor $\mW(\mI - \vx\vx\T)$ of \cref{eq:c11-dgd}.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{Reference implementation}\label{sec:c11-nl-ref}

\Cref{lst:c11-cms-ref} is a minimal three-level CMS with online writes, following
\cref{alg:c11-cms}. It uses the plain inner-product objective
$\sum_t\inner{\bm{\delta}_t}{\hat\vy_t}$, whose gradient $\sum_t\mathbf{J}_t\T\bm{\delta}_t$ is half
that of the shaped loss of \cref{lst:c11-chunkloss}, and plain SGD per level.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Reference implementation (ours): a three-level Continuum Memory System with a multi-frequency write schedule},label={lst:c11-cms-ref}]
import torch, torch.nn as nn

class Level(nn.Module):                      # one CMS level: x + MLP(x)
    def __init__(self, d, h):
        super().__init__()
        self.up, self.down = nn.Linear(d, h), nn.Linear(h, d)
    def forward(self, x):
        return x + self.down(torch.nn.functional.gelu(self.up(x)))

class CMS(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, periods=(8, 32, 128), lrs=(1e-2, 3e-3, 1e-3), h_mult=4):
        super().__init__()
        self.levels = nn.ModuleList(Level(d, h_mult * d) for _ in periods)
        self.periods, self.lrs = periods, lrs
        self.buf = [[] for _ in periods]     # (inputs, deltas) awaiting a write

    @torch.no_grad()
    def step(self, x, delta):
        """x, delta: (C_min, d) for one base chunk. Read, then write due levels."""
        h, ins = x, []
        for lvl in self.levels:              # read with current parameters
            ins.append(h)
            h = lvl(h)
        for l, (lvl, C, lr) in enumerate(zip(self.levels, self.periods, self.lrs)):
            self.buf[l].append((ins[l], delta))
            n = sum(z.shape[0] for z, _ in self.buf[l])
            if n < C:
                continue                     # level l is not due yet
            Z = torch.cat([z for z, _ in self.buf[l]])[:C]
            D = torch.cat([g for _, g in self.buf[l]])[:C]
            self.buf[l] = []                 # (assumes C is a multiple of C_min)
            with torch.enable_grad():        # one parallel gradient over the chunk
                params = list(lvl.parameters())
                obj = (lvl(Z) * D).sum()     # sum_t <delta_t, MLP(z_t)>
                grads = torch.autograd.grad(obj, params)
            for p, g in zip(params, grads):  # level-specific optimizer (SGD here)
                p -= lr * g
        return h

# streaming use: delta_t is the error signal at the block output
cms = CMS(d=64)
for x_chunk, d_chunk in zip(torch.randn(16, 8, 64), torch.randn(16, 8, 64)):
    y_chunk = cms.step(x_chunk, 1e-2 * d_chunk)
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:c11-nl-discussion}

\paragraph{Strengths.} Nested Learning gives one vocabulary for things usually treated
separately: attention, recurrent states, test-time training, optimizer states and pre-training
are all memories differing in context flow, objective and frequency. That lens produces concrete
designs. The CMS turns the period $C^{(\ell)}$ into a single, interpretable knob for the
timescale of a block of persistent memory, and the chunkwise form keeps it parallel. M3 shows the
same idea inside an optimizer. HOPE combines the most plastic memory (self-modifying Titans, whose
keys, values and learning rates themselves adapt in context) with a graded persistent memory.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The evidence is thin: there is no official code; the main-text table
shows HOPE close to Titans (better average accuracy at both sizes, worse perplexity at 760M); the
reproduction does not attempt result parity. The mechanism is intricate. Writes inside the
forward pass require two-pass training with stop-gradient writes, so the outer level does not
learn \emph{through} the inner writes unless one pays for the experimental differentiable mode;
TTT-E2E (\cref{ch:ttt}) instead meta-learns through its inner loop. Online writes during
inference mean that a model's behaviour depends on what it has read, and the reproduction's
per-context ``fast state'' is shared across a batch unless the batch size is $1$; when such
writes should be reset, and whether slow levels should persist across users, are exactly the
``consistency across episodes'' questions of the survey (\S IV-E(d)). Finally, a slow CMS level
written with plain gradients is still exposed to interference: sums over long chunks average out
noise but do not protect old knowledge, which is where the geometric methods of the next two
sections come in.

\paragraph{Relations.} The self-modifying memory is a Titans memory (\cref{ch:ttt}) whose
projections are also fast weights; its weight update is the delta rule of \cref{ch:delta}.
Log-Linear Attention (\cref{ch:structured}) also keeps memories at several timescales, but as a
hierarchy of recurrent \emph{states} over a Fenwick tree rather than as parameters written at
different frequencies; Memory Caching (\cref{ch:routing}) checkpoints recurrent memories at
segment boundaries.

\section{OLieRA: orthogonal low-rank adaptation in Lie groups}\label{sec:c11-olieRA}
% ===========================================================================

\begin{pitfall}[title={No public code}]
We found no code for OLieRA \citep{cao2025olora}. This section explains the method from the
survey's description (\S IV-E(f)) and from what the authors state publicly in their abstract.
Where we write equations beyond that, they are \emph{our formalization}; the paper's exact
parameterization may differ.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Motivation}\label{sec:c11-ol-motivation}

The systems of \cref{sec:c11-nested,ch:ttt} write memory parameters online. A slower but equally
important kind of write is the sequential fine-tuning of a deployed LLM on a stream of tasks:
each task writes new knowledge into the backbone, the model's long-term parametric memory, and
each write can erase earlier ones. O-LoRA (\cref{sec:c11-lora}) limits interference by giving
every task its own low-rank adapter in a subspace orthogonal to earlier adapters. The OLieRA
abstract identifies a remaining problem: such updates are \emph{additive}, and, the authors argue,
additive updates distort the intrinsic geometry of the pretrained parameters. OLieRA instead writes
each task as a \emph{multiplicative} update inside a Lie group, which preserves that geometry, and
keeps orthogonality across task subspaces. It keeps O-LoRA's practical properties: no replay
buffer and no task identifier at inference. The survey places it under geometric constraints for
parametric stability, as ``constraining low-rank updates on structured manifolds''. Such methods
are not explicit memory modules, but the survey notes that ``as more architectures introduce
writable parameter subsets, the geometry of the update rule may become as important as the
storage form itself'' (\S IV-E(f)).

\begin{taxonomy}
OLieRA is not in Table~I or Table~II of \citet{zhoubian2026memory}; the survey discusses it in
\S IV-E(f) as an adaptation method relevant to the stability of long-term parametric memory. Our
placement: \textbf{representation} implicit knowledge in the backbone weights, written through
explicit per-task parameter subsets (the adapters); \textbf{update dynamics} offline (task by task
during fine-tuning, fixed at inference); \textbf{persistence} long-term; \textbf{update rule}
optimization-based writing under a geometric (Lie-group and orthogonality) constraint.
\end{taxonomy}

\subsection{Formulation}\label{sec:c11-ol-formulation}

Public descriptions of OLieRA build the Lie group from tensors with no zero entries under the
Hadamard product, perform updates in the Lie algebra, and map them back with the exponential map
(\cref{sec:c11-lie}); they mention first- and second-order Taylor expansions of the exponential.
One way to formalize this for a weight $\mW_0 \in \mathcal{G}_{\had}$ and a task sequence
$t = 1,\dots,N$ is
\begin{equation}
  \mW_t = \mW_{t-1}\had\exp_{\had}(\mB_t\mA_t),
  \qquad \mB_t \in \R^{m\times r},\ \mA_t \in \R^{r\times n},
  \label{eq:c11-olie-update}
\end{equation}
where each task's Lie-algebra element $\bm{\Delta}_t = \mB_t\mA_t$ is low-rank, exactly as in LoRA,
with the same $r(m+n)$ trainable numbers. Four consequences follow directly from the group
structure.

\paragraph{Composition is addition in the algebra.} Because $\mathcal{G}_{\had}$ is commutative,
\begin{equation}
  \mW_N = \mW_0\had\exp_{\had}\Bigl(\sum_{t=1}^N\mB_t\mA_t\Bigr),
  \qquad
  \log\abs{\mW_N} = \log\abs{\mW_0} + \sum_{t=1}^N\mB_t\mA_t ,
  \label{eq:c11-olie-compose}
\end{equation}
independent of the order of the tasks, and the sign of every entry of $\mW_0$ is kept forever.
In log-magnitude coordinates OLieRA is an additive low-rank method; in weight coordinates it is
multiplicative.

\paragraph{The additive change is entry-proportional.} Expanding
$\exp_{\had}(\bm{\Delta}) = \bm{1} + \bm{\Delta} + \tfrac12\bm{\Delta}\had\bm{\Delta} + \dots$,
\begin{equation}
  \mW\had\exp_{\had}(\bm{\Delta}) = \mW + \mW\had\bm{\Delta} + \tfrac12\mW\had\bm{\Delta}\had\bm{\Delta} + \bigO(\norm{\bm{\Delta}}^3).
  \label{eq:c11-olie-taylor}
\end{equation}
To first order the weight moves by $\mW\had\bm{\Delta}$: every entry changes in proportion to its own
size. Large weights move more in absolute terms, small weights less, and no entry can cross
zero. This is the concrete sense in which, in our formalization, the update ``respects'' the
pretrained parameters: it changes their magnitudes by relative factors $e^{\Delta_{ij}}$ instead
of adding a pattern that ignores them.

\paragraph{Low rank in the algebra is not low rank in the weights.} For a rank-one element,
$\mW\had(\vb\va\T) = \diag(\vb)\,\mW\diag(\va)$: a rescaling of rows and columns, which has the
same rank as $\mW$ whenever $\va$ and $\vb$ have no zero entries. For rank $r$, $\mW\had(\mB\mA) = \sum_{j=1}^r\diag(\vb_j)\,\mW\diag(\va_j)$, where
$\vb_j$ and $\va_j\T$ are the columns of $\mB$ and rows of $\mA$. So a rank-$r$ multiplicative
adapter produces a first-order additive change of rank up to $\min(m,n)$ with only $r(m+n)$
parameters. (Our NumPy check: for a random $20\times16$ $\mW$ and $r \in \{1,2,4\}$ both
$\mW\had(\mB\mA)$ and $\mW\had(\exp_{\had}(\mB\mA) - \bm{1})$ have full rank $16$.)

\paragraph{Gradients in the algebra.} Write $\mW_{\text{eff}} = \mW\had\exp_{\had}(\mB\mA)$ and let
$\mG = \partial\loss/\partial\mW_{\text{eff}}$. Each entry of $\mW_{\text{eff}}$ depends only on the
matching entry of $\bm{\Delta} = \mB\mA$, with derivative
$\partial W_{\text{eff},ij}/\partial\Delta_{ij} = W_{\text{eff},ij}$, so the chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\loss}{\partial\bm{\Delta}} = \mG\had\mW_{\text{eff}}, \qquad
  \nabla_{\mB} = (\mG\had\mW_{\text{eff}})\mA\T, \qquad
  \nabla_{\mA} = \mB\T(\mG\had\mW_{\text{eff}}).
  \label{eq:c11-olie-grad}
\end{equation}
The Euclidean gradient is reweighted entry by entry by the current weights, as in multiplicative
(exponentiated-gradient) updates. With the LoRA initialization $\mB = \bm{0}$, $\mW_{\text{eff}} =
\mW$ at the start of each task, $\nabla_{\mA} = \bm{0}$ and $\nabla_{\mB} \neq \bm{0}$, as in LoRA.

\paragraph{Orthogonality across tasks.} To limit interference, the update of task $t$ is kept
orthogonal to those of earlier tasks. Secondary descriptions of the paper say that, unlike O-LoRA,
which constrains the low-rank factors, OLieRA enforces orthogonality on the \emph{entire} updated
parameter subspaces, represented through $\exp(\Delta\mW_t)$. We do not know its exact form. A
simple version that is cheap to compute, and that we use in our reference implementation, applies
the O-LoRA penalty of \cref{eq:c11-olora} in the Lie algebra:
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mB_t, \mA_t}\; \loss_t\bigl(\mW_{t-1}\had\exp_{\had}(\mB_t\mA_t)\bigr) + \lambda\sum_{s<t}\norm{\mA_s\mA_t\T}_F^2 .
  \label{eq:c11-olie-objective}
\end{equation}
This choice has a weakness, which may be one reason the paper constrains more than the factors. For an
additive adapter, $\mA_s\mA_t\T = \bm{0}$ guarantees $\mB_t\mA_t\vx = \bm{0}$ on old input directions
$\vx \in \operatorname{row}(\mA_s)$ (\cref{sec:c11-lora}). For the multiplicative adapter the
first-order change acts as $(\mW\had\mB_t\mA_t)\vx = \sum_j\vb_j\had\bigl(\mW(\va_j\had\vx)\bigr)$:
it depends on the element-wise products $\va_j\had\vx$, not on the inner products $\va_j\T\vx$.
Factor orthogonality therefore no longer implies non-interference (in our check the output change
on such an $\vx$ has norm $3.5$, against $2\times10^{-15}$ for the additive adapter).

\subsection{Algorithm and complexity}\label{sec:c11-ol-alg}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Continual fine-tuning with multiplicative low-rank updates (our formalization of OLieRA).}\label{alg:c11-olie}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require pretrained weights $\{\mW\}$ of the adapted layers; tasks $1..N$; rank $r$; penalty $\lambda$
\For{$t = 1,\dots,N$}
  \State $\mB_t \gets \bm{0}$, $\mA_t \gets$ small random \Comment{start at $\mW_{\text{eff}} = \mW$}
  \For{each mini-batch of task $t$}
    \State $\mW_{\text{eff}} \gets \mW\had\exp_{\had}(\mB_t\mA_t)$ \Comment{or the Taylor form of \cref{eq:c11-olie-taylor}}
    \State $\loss \gets \loss_t(\mW_{\text{eff}}) + \lambda\sum_{s<t}\norm{\mA_s\mA_t\T}_F^2$
    \State update $\mB_t, \mA_t$ with \cref{eq:c11-olie-grad} and any optimizer
  \EndFor
  \State $\mW \gets \mW\had\exp_{\had}(\mB_t\mA_t)$; store $\mA_t$ for later penalties \Comment{merge}
\EndFor
\Ensure merged weights; inference needs no adapters and no task identifier
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Cost.} Unlike LoRA, the multiplicative adapter cannot be applied as $\mB(\mA\vx)$:
the effective weight must be formed, $\bigO(mnr)$ for $\mB\mA$ plus $\bigO(mn)$ element-wise work
per layer and step, and the backward pass of \cref{eq:c11-olie-grad} costs another $\bigO(mnr)$
and an $m\times n$ temporary. Training memory therefore scales like full fine-tuning for the
activations of the weight, while optimizer state stays at LoRA's $r(m+n)$. The penalty costs
$\bigO(r^2n)$ per earlier task; storing old factors costs $\bigO(Nrn)$. After merging, inference
costs nothing extra.

\subsection{Reference implementation}\label{sec:c11-ol-ref}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Reference implementation (ours): a multiplicative low-rank adapter in the Hadamard Lie group with an O-LoRA-style orthogonality penalty},label={lst:c11-olie-ref}]
import torch, torch.nn as nn

class HadamardLieAdapter(nn.Module):
    """W_eff = W (.) exp(B A): low-rank update in the Lie algebra of
    (nonzero matrices, Hadamard product). Our formalization, not the paper's code."""
    def __init__(self, W0, r=8, order=None):
        super().__init__()
        m, n = W0.shape
        self.register_buffer("W", W0.clone())          # frozen; no entry may be 0
        self.B = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(m, r))       # LoRA-style start: W_eff = W
        self.A = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(r, n) / n ** 0.5)
        self.order = order                             # None = exact exp, 1 or 2 = Taylor
        self.old_A = []                                # factors of earlier tasks

    def effective_weight(self):
        D = self.B @ self.A                            # element of the Lie algebra
        if self.order == 1:
            return self.W * (1 + D)
        if self.order == 2:
            return self.W * (1 + D + 0.5 * D * D)
        return self.W * torch.exp(D)                   # exponential map, element-wise

    def forward(self, x):                              # x: (..., n)
        return x @ self.effective_weight().T

    def orth_penalty(self):                            # sum_s ||A_s A_t^T||_F^2
        return sum(((A_s @ self.A.T) ** 2).sum() for A_s in self.old_A)

    @torch.no_grad()
    def finish_task(self):                             # merge into W, start a new task
        self.W.mul_(torch.exp(self.B @ self.A))        # group product: signs kept
        self.old_A.append(self.A.detach().clone())
        self.B.zero_()
        self.A.normal_(0.0, self.A.shape[1] ** -0.5)

# per task: loss = task_loss(layer) + lam * layer.orth_penalty(); after it: layer.finish_task()
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Numerical checks.} Besides the checks quoted above, NumPy confirms that
$\mW\had e^{\bm{\Delta}_1}\had e^{\bm{\Delta}_2} = \mW\had e^{\bm{\Delta}_1+\bm{\Delta}_2}$ in either order (to
$5\times10^{-15}$), with every sign of $\mW$ preserved.

\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:c11-ol-discussion}

\paragraph{Strengths.} Multiplicative updates give each task a relative, sign-preserving change
of every weight from only $r(m+n)$ parameters, which is more expressive than an additive rank-$r$
update. The group structure makes task updates commute and compose by addition in the algebra,
which is a clean basis for reasoning about many tasks, and merged weights cost nothing at
inference.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The Hadamard group excludes zeros: a weight that is exactly zero (after
pruning or some quantization schemes) can never move, and a weight near zero can barely move, so
the method assumes that small weights should stay small. Training is more expensive than LoRA
because the full effective weight is formed. Orthogonality in the algebra does not by itself
imply functional non-interference, as shown above. Most important for this book, the evidence
consists of an abstract and secondary descriptions; with no code, the exact constraint cannot be
checked.

\paragraph{Relations.} OLieRA protects the backbone's long-term memory by \emph{where} it lets
updates go (orthogonal subspaces) and \emph{how} they act (multiplicatively). Muon-OGD, next,
protects it by choosing the geometry of the step itself. Both address the survey's question of
how to write long-lived parametric memory without drift (\S VI-B, \S VI-C).

\subsection{Motivation}\label{sec:c11-mo-motivation}

OGD (\cref{sec:c11-cl}) protects old tasks by projecting the gradient, a construction that lives
in Euclidean (Frobenius) geometry: it treats a weight matrix as a vector. The success of Muon
(\cref{sec:c11-muon}) suggests that, for matrix-valued LLM parameters, the spectral norm is a
better geometry for steps. Muon-OGD combines the two. According to its abstract, it formulates
each update as an optimization problem with a spectral-norm constraint and linear
non-interference constraints, solves it with dual iterations and Newton--Schulz approximations of
the matrix sign, and so applies orthogonalized momentum updates that avoid protected directions
associated with earlier tasks. The survey describes it as ``imposing spectral-norm-aware
orthogonal gradient projections'' (\S IV-E(f)).

\begin{taxonomy}
Not in Table~I or Table~II of \citet{zhoubian2026memory}; discussed in \S IV-E(f) as a constrained
update rule for long-term parametric memory. Our placement: \textbf{representation} implicit
(knowledge in the backbone weights); \textbf{update dynamics} offline (sequential fine-tuning
stages); \textbf{persistence} long-term; \textbf{update rule} optimization-based writing with
linear non-interference constraints in spectral geometry.
\end{taxonomy}

\subsection{Formulation}\label{sec:c11-mo-formulation}

For one weight matrix $\mW \in \R^{m\times n}$ with (momentum) gradient $\mG$ and protected
directions $\mC_1,\dots,\mC_k \in \R^{m\times n}$, the step solves
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\bm{\Delta}}\; \inner{\mG}{\bm{\Delta}}
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \norm{\bm{\Delta}}_2 \le \eta, \qquad \inner{\mC_i}{\bm{\Delta}} = 0,\ i = 1,\dots,k .
  \label{eq:c11-mo-primal}
\end{equation}
It is a convex problem: a linear objective, a norm ball and affine constraints.

\paragraph{In Frobenius geometry this is OGD.} Replace $\norm{\cdot}_2$ by $\norm{\cdot}_F$ and let
$\mP$ be the orthogonal projector onto $\{\bm{\Delta} : \inner{\mC_i}{\bm{\Delta}} = 0\ \forall i\}$. For
feasible $\bm{\Delta}$, $\mP\bm{\Delta} = \bm{\Delta}$, so $\inner{\mG}{\bm{\Delta}} = \inner{\mP\mG}{\bm{\Delta}} \ge
-\eta\norm{\mP\mG}_F$, with equality at $\bm{\Delta}^\star = -\eta\,\mP\mG/\norm{\mP\mG}_F$: the
projected, normalized gradient of \cref{eq:c11-ogd}.

\paragraph{In spectral geometry, project-then-orthogonalize fails.} The natural guess
$-\eta\operatorname{msign}(\mP\mG)$ is generally \emph{infeasible}: orthogonalization rotates the
step back into protected directions. In our NumPy check with $k = 3$ rank-one constraints on a
$12\times8$ matrix, $\mP\mG$ satisfies the constraints to $10^{-15}$ but
$\operatorname{msign}(\mP\mG)$ violates them by up to $0.75$. Two ways out follow.

\paragraph{Dual ascent.} Attach multipliers $\lambda_i$ to the equalities. The Lagrangian is
$\inner{\mH(\bm{\lambda})}{\bm{\Delta}}$ with $\mH(\bm{\lambda}) = \mG + \sum_i\lambda_i\mC_i$. For fixed
$\bm{\lambda}$, minimizing over the spectral ball is the unconstrained Muon problem of
\cref{eq:c11-nuclear}:
\begin{equation}
  \bm{\Delta}(\bm{\lambda}) = -\eta\operatorname{msign}\bigl(\mH(\bm{\lambda})\bigr),
  \qquad
  g(\bm{\lambda}) = -\eta\norm{\mG + \textstyle\sum_i\lambda_i\mC_i}_* .
  \label{eq:c11-mo-dual}
\end{equation}
The dual function $g$ is concave (a negated norm of an affine function). Where $\mH$ has full
rank, the nuclear norm is differentiable with gradient
$\operatorname{msign}(\mH)$, so $\partial g/\partial\lambda_i = -\eta\inner{\mC_i}{\operatorname{msign}(\mH)}$:
the dual gradient is exactly the constraint violation of the current primal step. Dual gradient
ascent with step $\eta_\lambda$ (absorbing $\eta$) is
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_i \leftarrow \lambda_i - \eta_\lambda\,\inner{\mC_i}{\operatorname{msign}(\mH(\bm{\lambda}))} .
  \label{eq:c11-mo-ascent}
\end{equation}
Strong duality holds by Slater's condition ($\bm{\Delta} = \bm{0}$ is feasible and strictly inside the
ball), so if $\mH(\bm{\lambda}^*)$ has full rank at the dual optimum $\bm{\lambda}^*$, the primal
minimizer there is unique, feasible and optimal. Each dual iteration costs
one matrix sign, which Newton--Schulz computes. For $k^2$ \emph{bilinear} constraints
$\mU\T\bm{\Delta}\mV = \bm{0}$ with $\mU \in \R^{m\times k}$, $\mV \in \R^{n\times k}$, the multiplier
is a matrix $\mLambda \in \R^{k\times k}$, $\mH = \mG + \mU\mLambda\mV\T$, and the ascent step is
$\mLambda \leftarrow \mLambda - \eta_\lambda\,\mU\T\operatorname{msign}(\mH)\mV$. In our check the
violation of rank-one constraints falls from $0.50$ ($T = 0$) to $0.43$, $0.096$ and
$1.6\times10^{-9}$ after $T = 10$, $100$ and $1000$ iterations, and at convergence the primal value
equals the dual value ($-20.2967$); the unconstrained Muon step reaches $-20.914$, so protection
costs about $3\%$ of the first-order decrease in this example. The bilinear version converges in
the same way ($\norm{\mU\T\bm{\Delta}\mV}_F$ from $0.71$ to $7\times10^{-15}$).

\paragraph{Closed form for one-sided protection.} If whole subspaces are protected on one side,
no iteration is needed.

\begin{proposition}[One-sided constraints]\label{prop:c11-onesided}
Let $\mU \in \R^{m\times k}$ have orthonormal columns and $\mP = \mI - \mU\mU\T$. The solution of
$\min\inner{\mG}{\bm{\Delta}}$ subject to $\norm{\bm{\Delta}}_2 \le \eta$ and $\mU\T\bm{\Delta} = \bm{0}$ is
$\bm{\Delta}^\star = -\eta\operatorname{msign}(\mP\mG)$. Symmetrically, with $\mV \in \R^{n\times k}$
orthonormal and the constraint $\bm{\Delta}\mV = \bm{0}$, it is $-\eta\operatorname{msign}(\mG(\mI - \mV\mV\T))$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Feasibility: write the thin SVD $\mP\mG = \tilde\mU\tilde\mSigma\tilde\mV\T$ over the nonzero
singular values. Every column of $\tilde\mU$ lies in the range of $\mP\mG$, hence in the range of
$\mP$, so $\mU\T\tilde\mU = \bm{0}$ and $\mU\T\operatorname{msign}(\mP\mG) = \mU\T\tilde\mU\tilde\mV\T = \bm{0}$.
Optimality: any feasible $\bm{\Delta}$ has $\mP\bm{\Delta} = \bm{\Delta}$, so
$\inner{\mG}{\bm{\Delta}} = \inner{\mG}{\mP\bm{\Delta}} = \inner{\mP\mG}{\bm{\Delta}} \ge -\eta\norm{\mP\mG}_*$ by
\cref{eq:c11-nuclear}, and $\bm{\Delta}^\star$ attains this bound. The right-sided case is the
transpose.
\end{proof}

The right-sided case is exactly the GPM projection of \cref{eq:c11-gpm} followed by Muon's
orthogonalization: \emph{input-subspace} protection is compatible with spectral steps. The proof
relies on $\operatorname{msign}$ mapping zero singular values to zero; an SVD-based
$\operatorname{msign}$ that keeps them reintroduces arbitrary directions (our first NumPy attempt
did exactly that), whereas Newton--Schulz keeps them at zero automatically.

\paragraph{Which directions to protect.} The three constraint types protect increasingly much.
A rank-one constraint $\inner{\sigma_i\vu_i\vv_i\T}{\bm{\Delta}} = \sigma_i\vu_i\T\bm{\Delta}\vv_i = 0$ built
from a singular triplet of $\mW$ has a clean meaning. Differentiating $\sigma_i = \vu_i\T\mW\vv_i$
and using $\mW\vv_i = \sigma_i\vu_i$, $\mW\T\vu_i = \sigma_i\vv_i$ and $\vu_i\T d\vu_i = \vv_i\T d\vv_i = 0$
(unit vectors) gives $d\sigma_i = \vu_i\T(d\mW)\vv_i$. So such a constraint keeps the $i$-th
singular value of the layer unchanged to first order. Bilinear constraints $\mU_k\T\bm{\Delta}\mV_k =
\bm{0}$ additionally freeze the interactions between the top-$k$ singular pairs; one-sided constraints
freeze a whole output (or input) subspace and leave the least room for learning. The repository
builds protected directions from the top-$k$ singular triplets of the weights at the start of a
stage (default) or of gradients accumulated on replayed old-task examples.

\subsection{Algorithm and complexity}\label{sec:c11-mo-alg}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One Muon-OGD step for a weight matrix (rank-one constraints, warm-started duals).}\label{alg:c11-muonogd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\mW$, gradient $\mG$, momentum $\mM$, protected $\mC_i = \sigma_i\vu_i\vv_i\T$ ($i\le k$),
duals $\bm{\lambda}$ (kept across steps), rates $\eta, \eta_\lambda$, dual iterations $T$
\State $\mM \gets \mu\mM + \mG$ \Comment{optional momentum}
\For{$\tau = 1,\dots,T$}
  \State $\mS \gets \operatorname{msign}(\mM + \sum_i\lambda_i\mC_i)$ \Comment{Newton--Schulz}
  \State $\lambda_i \gets \lambda_i - \eta_\lambda\,\sigma_i\vu_i\T\mS\vv_i$ for all $i$ \Comment{constraint violations}
\EndFor
\State $\mW \gets (1 - \eta\,\mathrm{wd})\mW - \eta\operatorname{msign}(\mM + \sum_i\lambda_i\mC_i)$
\Ensure $\mW$, $\mM$, $\bm{\lambda}$. Cost $(T+1)$ matrix signs $+\ \bigO(kmn)$; state $\mM$, $k(m+n+1)$ factor numbers, $k$ duals
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Each step costs $T+1$ matrix signs, i.e.\ $(T+1)K$ Newton--Schulz iterations of
$\bigO(mn\min(m,n))$, plus $\bigO(kmn)$ to add the rank-one terms and evaluate the violations;
with $T = 1$ it is about twice the optimizer cost of Muon, still small next to the forward and
backward passes of a large batch. Extracting protected directions needs a truncated SVD once per
stage. With $T = 1$ the constraints are met only approximately at each step; warm-starting the
duals lets them accumulate across optimizer steps, so the duals act as a slowly updated memory of
how strongly each protected direction is being pushed.

\begin{implnote}[title={In the code: the matrix sign is not the polar factor as written}]
\code{_msgn} uses either an exact SVD (\code{U @ Vh}) or the coupled iteration of
\cref{lst:c11-osd-ns} (default \code{"ns"}, $4$ iterations from the command line, $6$ as the
function default). Unrolling it, $\mY_k = \mA\mZ_k$ and every matrix is a polynomial in
$\mA\T\mA$, so per eigenvalue $x = \sigma^2$ of $\mA\T\mA$ the scalars obey
$z \leftarrow \tfrac12 z(3 - z^3x)$, because $\mZ\mY\T\mY = \mZ^3\mA\T\mA$. The fixed point is
$z = x^{-1/3}$, so the output's singular values tend to $\sigma z = \sigma^{1/3}$, not to $1$: the
iteration converges to $\mU\tilde\mSigma^{1/3}\mV\T$ with $\tilde\mSigma$ the singular values of the
Frobenius-normalized input. The classical iteration \cref{eq:c11-ns3} uses $\mY\T\mY$ in place of
$\mZ\mY\T\mY$. We checked this by transcribing the update into NumPy (we did not run the
repository's code): on a $64\times32$ Gaussian matrix the singular values after $4$ iterations lie
in $[0.31, 0.65]$ and after $50$ iterations match $\tilde\sigma^{1/3}$ to $5\times10^{-9}$, with the
singular vectors exact. The result still compresses the spectrum (it maps a condition number
$\kappa$ to $\kappa^{1/3}$) and still has the right singular vectors, but it is not
$\operatorname{msign}$, the constraint violations it feeds to the dual step are those of a
different matrix, and the effective step is smaller than $\eta$. Whether the missing optimizer
class uses this routine cannot be checked.
\end{implnote}

\subsection{Reference implementation}\label{sec:c11-mo-ref}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Reference implementation (ours): a Muon step with Newton--Schulz orthogonalization and orthogonal protection, exact for one-sided subspaces and by dual ascent for general linear constraints},label={lst:c11-muonogd-ref}]
import torch

def newton_schulz(G, steps=20):
    """msign(G) = U V^T over nonzero singular values; zero ones stay zero."""
    X = G / (G.norm() + 1e-12)                 # singular values in (0, 1]
    tall = X.shape[0] >= X.shape[1]
    X = X if tall else X.T
    I = torch.eye(X.shape[1], dtype=X.dtype)
    for _ in range(steps):
        X = 0.5 * X @ (3 * I - X.T @ X)        # sigma -> 1.5 sigma - 0.5 sigma^3
    return X if tall else X.T

def muon_step_projected(W, M, G, U=None, V=None, lr=0.02, mu=0.95):
    """One-sided protection (Prop.): U^T dW = 0 (outputs) and/or dW V = 0 (inputs)."""
    M.mul_(mu).add_(G)                         # momentum: memory of past gradients
    H = M
    if U is not None:
        H = H - U @ (U.T @ H)                  # project out protected output subspace
    if V is not None:
        H = H - (H @ V) @ V.T                  # project out protected input subspace
    W.sub_(lr * newton_schulz(H))              # exact constrained steepest step
    return W, M

def muon_ogd_step(W, M, G, Cs, lam, lr=0.02, lr_dual=0.05, T=1, mu=0.95):
    """General constraints <C_i, dW> = 0 by dual ascent; lam is warm-started."""
    M.mul_(mu).add_(G)
    for _ in range(T):
        H = M + sum(l * C for l, C in zip(lam, Cs))
        S = newton_schulz(H)
        viol = torch.stack([(C * S).sum() for C in Cs])  # dual gradient
        lam = lam - lr_dual * viol
    H = M + sum(l * C for l, C in zip(lam, Cs))
    W.sub_(lr * newton_schulz(H))
    return W, M, lam
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Numerical checks.} Besides the results already quoted, NumPy confirms that cubic
Newton--Schulz maps zero singular values to zero (error $7\times10^{-10}$ against $\mU_r\mV_r\T$ on a
rank-$3$ $10\times6$ matrix), that both one-sided steps of \cref{prop:c11-onesided} satisfy their
constraints to $10^{-15}$ and attain $-\eta\norm{\mP\mG}_*$, and that warm-started duals ($T = 50$
per step) cut the violation from $0.23$ to about $0.01$--$0.02$ within five slowly drifting steps.

\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:c11-mo-discussion}

\paragraph{Strengths.} Muon-OGD states precisely what an update may and may not do: it is the best
first-order step in the geometry that works well for matrices, among steps that do not touch
protected directions. The dual view explains when projection is enough (one-sided subspaces,
\cref{prop:c11-onesided}) and when it is not, and the dual variables can be warm-started so that
the extra cost is one or two matrix signs per step.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Protection is only first order: the constraints remove the linear term of
\cref{eq:c11-taylor}, not the curvature term, so many small steps can still drift. Weight-SVD
directions are a proxy for what old tasks need; gradient-based directions need replayed data.
With $T = 1$ the constraints hold only approximately. The snapshot is incomplete and its default
matrix-sign routine, as written, computes a different matrix function. Finally, the protected set
faces the stability--plasticity dilemma again: if directions are accumulated over stages, the set
grows and plasticity shrinks; if they are recomputed from the latest weights, as the repository
does ($k$ per layer per stage), directions that mattered only for older tasks can drop out.

\paragraph{Relations.} Muon-OGD is OGD \citep{c11-farajtabar2020ogd} in Muon's geometry; with
input-side subspaces it reduces to GPM \citep{c11-saha2021gpm} plus orthogonalization. Its rank-one
constraints from singular triplets connect to the low-rank structure that OLieRA and O-LoRA
exploit, and the same constrained step could be used to write the slow levels of a CMS
(\cref{sec:c11-hybrid}).

\section{Multi-timescale hybridization}\label{sec:c11-hybrid}
% ===========================================================================

Hybrid models usually combine memories that differ in \emph{representation}, such as attention
and a recurrent state (\cref{ch:hybrid}). The survey notes that they also differ in \emph{update
timescale}: ``when integrated into larger architectures, these mechanisms create memory
hierarchies in which activation states evolve at token-level granularity, explicit memory modules
update at interaction or batch level, and backbone parameters remain frozen or adapt slowly''
(\S V-A(d)). \Cref{tab:c11-timescales} lays out this hierarchy with the systems of this book, and
\cref{fig:c11-tiers} draws it on a time axis.

\paragraph{The known systems as nested levels.} In the language of \cref{def:c11-frequency}, Titans
has three levels: attention over a window (non-parametric, every token), the neural memory
(\cref{eq:c11-titans}, every token, forgetting at rate $\alpha_t$), and the projections and
persistent-memory tokens (written in pre-training only). TTT-E2E \citep{tandon2025ttte2e} has two:
a sliding-window Transformer that ``continues learning at test time via next-token prediction on
the given context, compressing the context it reads into its weights'' (inner level, one step per
mini-batch of context), and a meta-learned initialization trained ``via meta-learning at training
time'' (outer level, one step per training batch). HOPE fills the gap between them with CMS levels,
so that information can be held for hundreds or thousands of tokens in parameters that are
neither reset with the episode nor frozen. OLieRA and Muon-OGD act at the slowest tier, where
writes are rare but must not erase.

\paragraph{How long does each tier remember?} By \cref{eq:c11-horizon}, a state with decay $\alpha$
per token remembers about $1/(1-\alpha)$ tokens, and a memory written every $C$ tokens with
retention $\beta$ per write about $C/(1-\beta)$ tokens ($C = 128$, $\beta = 0.99$: about $12{,}800$).
Without decay, interference from later writes, not decay, sets the horizon.

\paragraph{Design rules.} Putting the chapter together suggests five rules for multi-timescale
memory. (1) \emph{Separate timescales explicitly}, by update period and by decay, rather than
letting one memory serve all horizons. (2) \emph{Let slow levels average}: chunk sums
(\cref{eq:c11-cms-update}) and slow momenta (M3) filter out token-level noise. (3) \emph{Gate writes}
by a surprise signal, as Titans and the reproduction's surprise threshold do, so that slow levels
are written only when there is something to learn. (4) \emph{Constrain the geometry of slow
writes}, because slow levels hold long-lived knowledge; the constrained steps of OLieRA and
Muon-OGD are candidates for writing slow CMS levels. (5) \emph{Decide reset policy per tier}: which
levels are per-episode, per-user or global is the ``consistency across episodes'' question of
the survey (\S IV-E(d)), and it determines what an online memory may leak or forget.

\section{Comparison and discussion}\label{sec:c11-comparison}
% ===========================================================================

\Cref{tab:c11-compare} compares the three systems. They answer different halves of the chapter's
question: Nested Learning decides \emph{when} each part of a model is written; OLieRA and
Muon-OGD decide \emph{how} a write may move the parameters. They are complementary rather than
competing. The survey's open problems frame what is missing (\S VI-B, \S VI-C): update protocols
that are stable over long horizons, modular isolation of what each write may touch, consolidation
that moves knowledge from fast to slow levels, and update rules that are diagnosable and, where
needed, reversible. None of the three systems has yet been tested at the scale or with the
openness (official code, released checkpoints) of the recurrent and test-time-training models in
\cref{ch:delta,ch:ttt}.

\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Writable parametric memory raises two questions: how often each parameter group is written
  (its \emph{frequency}) and how a write may move parameters without erasing old content (its
  \emph{geometry}). Forgetting is a first-order term $\nabla\loss_j\T\bm{\Delta}$ plus curvature
  (\cref{eq:c11-taylor}).
  \item Every stateful component is an associative memory with a frequency and a decay: gradient
  descent writes input--error outer products, momentum is a decaying memory of gradients, Adam has
  two timescales.
  \item \textbf{Nested Learning} orders components into levels by update frequency; the
  \textbf{Continuum Memory System} chains MLP levels written every $C^{(\ell)}$ tokens with chunk sums
  of error terms, exactly parallel within a chunk (\cref{prop:c11-cms-chunk}); \textbf{HOPE} =
  self-modifying Titans + CMS. Only an unofficial, mechanism-level reproduction exists.
  \item \textbf{OLieRA} writes each task as $\mW \leftarrow \mW\had\exp(\mB\mA)$ in the Hadamard Lie
  group with orthogonality across tasks: sign-preserving, order-independent, full additive rank from
  $r(m+n)$ parameters. No code; our formalization.
  \item \textbf{Muon-OGD} takes the spectral-norm steepest step subject to $\inner{\mC_i}{\bm{\Delta}} = 0$,
  by dual ascent on $-\eta\norm{\mG + \sum_i\lambda_i\mC_i}_*$ with Newton--Schulz; one-sided
  constraints give $-\eta\operatorname{msign}(\mP\mG)$ (\cref{prop:c11-onesided}). The probably-official
  code is incomplete, and its default Newton--Schulz variant, as written, converges to
  $\mU\tilde\mSigma^{1/3}\mV\T$, not $\mU\mV\T$.
  \item Multi-timescale hybrids stack token-level states, interaction-level explicit memories and a
  slow backbone (\cref{tab:c11-timescales}); timescale separation and constrained geometry are
  complementary defences against the stability--plasticity dilemma.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Momentum as a memory]\label{exr:c11-momentum}
(a) Verify \cref{eq:c11-momentum-obj}. (b) Show that Adam's first moment
$\vm_t = \beta_1\vm_{t-1} + (1-\beta_1)\vg_t$ is one gradient step on
$\ell_t(\vm) = \tfrac12\norm{\vm - \vg_t}^2$ with rate $1-\beta_1$, and compute its mean lag with
\cref{eq:c11-horizon}. (c) M3 (\cref{lst:c11-m3}) adds a slow momentum refreshed every $C$ steps
from the sum of the last $C$ gradients. Write its impulse response (the weight a gradient from
$s$ steps ago receives in the update) and compare the two timescales.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Spectral steepest descent]\label{exr:c11-spectral}
(a) Prove $\abs{\vu\T\bm{\Delta}\vv} \le \norm{\bm{\Delta}}_2$ for unit $\vu,\vv$ and complete the proof
of \cref{eq:c11-nuclear}. (b) Find a $2\times2$ matrix $\mG$ and a single constraint matrix $\mC$
such that $\inner{\mC}{\mP\mG} = 0$ but $\inner{\mC}{\operatorname{msign}(\mP\mG)} \neq 0$, where
$\mP$ is the Frobenius projector onto $\mC^\perp$. (c) For two-sided constraints
$\mU\T\bm{\Delta} = \bm{0}$ \emph{and} $\bm{\Delta}\mV = \bm{0}$, show that
$-\eta\operatorname{msign}((\mI-\mU\mU\T)\mG(\mI-\mV\mV\T))$ is optimal.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Newton--Schulz]\label{exr:c11-ns}
(a) Show that the scalar map $\varphi(\sigma) = \tfrac32\sigma - \tfrac12\sigma^3$ sends
$(0,\sqrt3)$ into $(0, 1]$ after one step from any $\sigma \in (0,1]$ and converges quadratically
to $1$. Estimate how many steps a singular value $\sigma = 10^{-3}$ needs. (b) For the coupled
iteration of \cref{lst:c11-osd-ns}, prove that $\mY_k = \mA\mZ_k$, derive the scalar recursion
$z \leftarrow \tfrac12z(3 - z^3x)$, and show that its fixed point $x^{-1/3}$ is attracting
($\abs{\text{derivative}} = \tfrac12$). Modify one line so that the iteration converges to the polar
factor.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Lie groups]\label{exr:c11-lie}
(a) Show that $\det\exp(\mA) = e^{\tr\mA}$ for diagonalizable $\mA$, and conclude that $\exp$ maps
skew-symmetric matrices into $\mathrm{SO}(n)$. (b) Compute $\exp(\mA) - \operatorname{cay}(\mA)$ to
third order. (c) In the Hadamard group, prove $\rank(\mW\had\mB\mA) \le r\,\rank(\mW)$ using
$\mW\had(\vb\va\T) = \diag(\vb)\mW\diag(\va)$. (d) Propose an orthogonality penalty for
multiplicative adapters that does imply first-order non-interference on a stored set of old
inputs $\{\vx\}$, and give its cost.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Coding: forgetting across timescales]\label{exr:c11-coding}
Using \cref{lst:c11-cms-ref}, build a one-layer CMS with periods $(1, 16, 256)$ that is trained
online to map random keys to values (error signal $\bm{\delta}_t = \hat\vy_t - \vv_t$). Stream $5000$
pairs from association set A, then $5000$ from set B. Measure the recall error on A over time for
(i) the full CMS, (ii) only the fastest level, (iii) only the slowest level. Then replace the plain
SGD write of the slowest level by the input-side projected Muon step of
\cref{lst:c11-muonogd-ref}, protecting the top-$4$ principal directions of the A keys, and repeat.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Dual ascent]\label{exr:c11-dual}
Implement \code{muon_ogd_step} from \cref{lst:c11-muonogd-ref} in NumPy with an exact SVD-based
$\operatorname{msign}$. For random $64\times32$ $\mG$ and $k = 4$ random constraints, plot the
violation $\max_i\abs{\inner{\mC_i}{\bm{\Delta}}}$ and the duality gap against $T$ for several dual
steps $\eta_\lambda$. Then warm-start $\bm{\lambda}$ with $T = 1$ over slowly drifting gradients
$\mG_s = \mG + 0.05\,\mathbf{N}_s$ ($\mathbf{N}_s$ Gaussian) and report when the violation falls
below $10^{-3}$.
\end{exercise}
